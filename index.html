Stop I · The Charles River Basin

Built from Water

1630s – 1910

Your guide: a Head of the Charles rower, on a slow cool-down paddle the morning after the race.

The river, this morning

Easy — let the boat run. This is my favorite part of race weekend. The crowds have gone home, and it's just the oars and the water.

Most people think of the Charles as something that flows past Boston. It's closer to the opposite. Boston is something that was poured into the Charles.

Before Boston

Long before any of this, the Massachusett people fished these waters and called the river Quinobequin — "the meandering one." The hilly peninsula up ahead was Shawmut: under 800 acres of land, tied to the mainland by a neck of sand so thin that high tides nearly swept over it.

By the time English ships arrived, epidemics carried by European traders had already killed most of the Massachusett living here.

1630

John Winthrop's Puritans landed first in Charlestown, but they were short on fresh water. A lone Englishman, William Blaxton, was already living on Shawmut beside a good spring, and he invited them across.

They named the place Boston, after a town in Lincolnshire. For a while they also called it Trimountaine, for its three steep hills. See them? Hold on to that picture — it's about to change.

1800s · Moving the hills

As the town grew, it ran out of room. So Bostonians did something remarkable: they cut the tops off their own hills and dumped them into the water.

Beacon Hill lost roughly sixty feet. That earth filled the old Mill Pond, which is now the streets around North Station. The hills went down, and the shoreline crept out.

1857 – 1882 · The Back Bay

The biggest job was the Back Bay. A mill dam had cut that tidal flat off from the sea, and it had turned into a stinking marsh full of sewage. In 1857 the city started filling it in.

Trains hauled gravel from Needham, nine miles west, day and night for decades. Every brownstone on Commonwealth Avenue stands on what used to be the bottom of a bay.

1910 · Still water

Even this calm water isn't natural. In 1910 a dam near the river's mouth shut out the tide, turning a muddy estuary into a basin smooth enough to race on.

By then Boston had nearly tripled in size. So when I pull this oar, I'm rowing along the edge of a city that was poured into the river. Keep drifting toward the harbor. The old waterfront is up ahead, though these days it's on dry land.

— the rower

The fog lifts, and the light goes out of the sky…

Downstream · December 1773

Stop II · Griffin's Wharf

The Boston Tea Party

December 16, 1773

Your guide: a Red Sox fan who wanders off from the ballpark and onto the old harbor line.

Leaving Fenway

Night game's over and I'm not ready to go home, so I walk. Most people in those seats don't know this, but Fenway sits on the Fens, an old salt marsh that got filled in just like the Back Bay.

By the time I reach Congress Street, I'm standing on land that was open harbor in 1773. Griffin's Wharf was right about here. Picture it with me: three ships tied up, and a town full of very angry people.

1773 · Tea and taxes

That spring, Parliament passed the Tea Act. It actually made tea cheaper, but it kept a tax the colonists had never agreed to, and it handed the trade to a single company. To a lot of Bostonians, cheap taxed tea was a trap: drink it, and you'd accepted Parliament's right to tax you.

In late November the Dartmouth tied up at Griffin's Wharf, and the Eleanor and the Beaver soon followed. By law, their cargo had to be unloaded and taxed within twenty days. The clock would run out on December 17.

December 16 · Old South Meeting House

On the last day, about five thousand people packed into Old South Meeting House and spilled out into the street. They waited while the Dartmouth's owner rode out to ask Governor Thomas Hutchinson to let the ships sail back to England, tax unpaid. Near sundown the answer came back: no.

As the story goes, Samuel Adams rose and said, "This meeting can do nothing more to save the country." Signal or not, the crowd started moving toward the water.

That night · On the ships

Somewhere between thirty and a hundred and thirty men boarded the three ships, some loosely disguised as Mohawk Indians with soot on their faces. For about three hours they hauled chests up from the holds, split them open with hatchets, and dumped them over the side: 342 chests, more than 90,000 pounds of tea.

The tide was low, so the tea piled up in the shallows like haystacks, and men had to climb down and push it under. They worked almost in silence and, by their own accounts, took nothing else.

1774 · The harbor closes

London's answer was fierce. The Boston Port Act shut the harbor to all shipping until the tea was paid for. In a town that lived off the water, that meant idle wharves and empty tables. The other colonies sent food overland, then sent delegates to the First Continental Congress. Seven months later, the shooting started at Lexington and Concord.

So the spark of the Revolution was an act of water. They didn't burn the tea. They gave it to the harbor, and that harbor is now dry land under the street I'm standing on.

— the Red Sox fan

Morning again. A starting gun echoes across the hills…

Downstream · 1847 – today

Stop III · The neighborhoods

Waves of Arrival

1847 – today

Your guide: a Boston Marathon runner, taking the city mile by mile.

Mile 0 · Hopkinton

It's 26.2 miles from here to Boylston Street. The first Boston Marathon, in 1897, had fifteen starters. Now it's about thirty thousand runners from more than a hundred countries, which feels right for this city.

Fair warning: the course doesn't go through the North End or Chinatown. But every mile of it runs past somebody's arrival story. I think about them when my legs start to hurt.

1847 · The Irish

When the potato blight struck Ireland, famine drove more than a million people from the country. In 1847 alone, about 37,000 Irish arrived in Boston, a city of only about 115,000. Most came with almost nothing and crowded into cellars and tenements along the waterfront.

They took the hardest work there was: digging, hauling, laying railroad track, and filling in the Back Bay from Stop I. Plenty of Boston didn't want them. In 1854 the anti-immigrant Know-Nothing party swept the state elections. Thirty years later, Boston elected its first Irish-born mayor.

1880s – 1920s · The Italians

Next came the Italians, mostly from southern Italy and Sicily, and many of them settled in the North End. Whole villages rebuilt themselves there, street by street. Neighbors from the same hometown lived on the same block and honored the same patron saint.

In 1873 they founded St. Leonard's, the first church built by Italian immigrants in New England. By the 1920s the neighborhood was overwhelmingly Italian, and the saints' feasts they started still parade through its narrow streets every summer.

Mile 24 · Brookline

Here's where the course and the history finally meet. Around 1900, Jewish families escaping persecution in Eastern Europe landed in the North End and the West End. Over the next decades they moved out to Roxbury and Dorchester, then to Brookline and Newton, right along the road I'm running.

Chinese workers, meanwhile, built Chinatown starting in the 1870s, on filled land at the old South Cove. They did it under the Chinese Exclusion Act, a federal law that for sixty years barred nearly all new immigrants from China.

Mile 26.2 · Boylston Street

The waves never stopped. Cape Verdeans, Haitians, Vietnamese, Dominicans and Salvadorans built their own corners of Dorchester, Mattapan, Jamaica Plain and East Boston. Back at mile 6, Framingham has one of the largest Brazilian communities in the country. Today, more than a quarter of Bostonians were born in another country.

And the finish line? Boylston Street, in the Back Bay, on land the Irish helped pour into the water. Every step of this race lands on something built by people who got here with next to nothing.

— the marathoner

The air turns hazy. A crowd roars somewhere nearby…

Downstream · November 1960

Stop IV · The Boston Garden

From Famine Ship to White House

1849 – 1961

Your guide: a Celtics player, dribbling out of the old Boston Garden on November 7, 1960. The team has won back-to-back titles, but tonight the Garden belongs to someone else.

November 7, 1960 · The Garden

We've won the last two championships, and tonight we still got bumped off our own floor. Every seat in the Garden is full and Causeway Street is jammed, and none of these people came for basketball. It's the last night of the presidential campaign, and the candidate came home to finish it here.

The country votes tomorrow. To see how a Boston kid got this far, you have to go back to the docks.

1849 · East Boston

His great-grandfather, Patrick Kennedy, was one of the famine Irish from Stop III. He left County Wexford, came ashore in East Boston in 1849, and worked as a barrel maker. He died of cholera at thirty-five, leaving a widow and four small children.

His son P.J. grew up poor, opened a saloon, and worked his way into politics. In this town, that's how Irish families climbed: one ward, one favor, one election at a time.

1916 · Honey Fitz vs. the Brahmins

His other grandfather was John "Honey Fitz" Fitzgerald, born in the North End and twice mayor of Boston. Old Boston was run by the Brahmins, Protestant Yankee families whose names were on the banks and the colleges. In 1916, Honey Fitz ran for the U.S. Senate against one of the grandest of them, Henry Cabot Lodge.

He lost. But the family had a long memory.

1946 – 1952 · The grandson

Jack Kennedy was born in Brookline in 1917. Home from the war in 1946, he won a seat in Congress representing the waterfront neighborhoods his family came from: East Boston, the North End, Charlestown.

In 1952 he ran for the Senate against Henry Cabot Lodge Jr. That made it Honey Fitz's grandson against the grandson of the man who had beaten him. Kennedy won by about seventy thousand votes. It took thirty-six years, but the immigrant side of the family had evened the score.

Tomorrow, and after

Tonight he's up on that stage. Tomorrow he'll be elected the first Catholic president, in one of the closest races in American history. Two months later, back in Boston, he'll tell the state legislature that the nation must be "as a city upon a hill." Those are John Winthrop's words from 1630, now spoken by the great-grandson of a famine immigrant.

Same city. Same hill. Very different crowd.

— the Celtics player

The haze burns off. The air turns cold and bright…

Downstream · 2021

Stop V · City Hall, by the harbor

A First for City Hall

November 2021

Your guide: a PWHL Boston Fleet player, skating on the harbor ice below City Hall.

The harbor, on skates

Professional women's hockey in Boston is still new. Our league dropped its first puck in 2024. So I think a lot about firsts, and how they take forever and then seem to happen all at once.

Look up past the waterfront. That concrete building that's wider at the top than at the bottom is City Hall. In 2021, something happened in there for the first time in two hundred years.

1822 – 2021 · Two centuries of mayors

Boston became a city in 1822. For the next two hundred years, every mayor the voters chose was a white man. First came the Yankee Brahmins. Then came the Irish: Hugh O'Brien, Honey Fitz from Stop IV, James Michael Curley. Irish American mayors ran the city for most of the twentieth century.

The city changed faster than City Hall did. By the 2000 census, most Bostonians were people of color, but the mayor's office still hadn't caught up.

Michelle Wu

Michelle Wu grew up in Chicago, the daughter of immigrants from Taiwan. She came to Boston for Harvard. When her mother became seriously ill, she took on caring for her and raising her younger sisters, while she was in law school. One of her professors and mentors there was Elizabeth Warren.

In 2013 she won a seat on the city council, the first Asian American woman ever to do it. Three years later her colleagues made her council president, the first woman of color in that job.

2021 · The race

In March 2021, Mayor Marty Walsh left to become U.S. Secretary of Labor. Council president Kim Janey stepped in as acting mayor, the first woman and the first Black Bostonian to hold the office. That fall, every major candidate for mayor was a woman, a person of color, or both, and the final two were both women.

On November 2, Boston voters chose Michelle Wu by nearly two to one. She was the first woman, and the first person of color, they had ever elected mayor.

Today · Back to the water

From out here on the ice, I can see the whole story at once: the harbor where the tea went in, the waterfront where the famine ships came ashore, the old wharves buried under landfill, and City Hall above it all.

Boston started as a hilly little peninsula that ran out of room. It made more land, and then, slowly and unevenly, more room for more people, one first at a time. There are still firsts left.

— the Fleet player

The Harbor

The boat drifts out past the harbor islands. One river, five guides, four centuries, and every one of these stories touched the water. Boston didn't just grow up beside it. The city was built out of it.

The guides are fictional. The history they tell is real.
